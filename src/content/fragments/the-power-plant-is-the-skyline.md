# things that are not there

you smell Lamma before you understand it

the ferry doors open at yung shue wan
and two hundred bodies push through a gangway built for forty,
and then you are inside the village with
no transition, no threshold, no breath between water and land
the narrow main street begins at the gangway
and there is nowhere to go but forward
pressed between someone's backpack and someone's laminated menu

the street is maybe two metres wide.
both sides are restaurant frontage, shop signage,
awnings extending so far overhead they form a tunnel.
ground floors sell dried fish and bicycle parts and Gatorade.
upper floors dry laundry on iron balconies.
a woman in a pink shirt walks a bicycle through the crowd
with the patience of someone who does this every day
and will do it again tomorrow.

three minutes from the pier,
between the first and second restaurant blocks,
there is a drainage channel running toward the harbour.
the water in it is not clear.
it has a greenish opacity and moves slowly,
and above it hangs a sweetness that is not food.

:::fact Sewage Infrastructure
Lamma operated for decades on roughly a thousand buried septic tanks. Some dated to the 1930s. Many stopped functioning due to lack of maintenance. Some homes pumped waste directly into the sea. The government approved PWP No. 4355DS in 2021 at a cost of HK$502.9 million, covering approximately 5 km of gravity sewers across seven previously unsewered areas and two new pumping stations. Completion is scheduled for mid 2026.
:::

the construction barriers now occupy so much of the only footpath
that in places you walk single file between orange plastic walls,
a concrete mixer on one side, wild grass on the other,
the ==power station chimneys=={fact: The Lamma Power Station was commissioned in 1982 by Hongkong Electric Company Limited. It has an installed capacity of 3,617 megawatts and supplies electricity to approximately 570,000 customers on Hong Kong Island. The station operates on a combination of coal and natural gas. Its three chimneys are each over 200 metres tall.}
rising ahead of you
like something from another century that forgot to leave.

those chimneys.
three of them, each over two hundred metres tall,
painted in pale bands that catch the light at dusk
and glow faintly against the green ridge of Mount Stenhouse.

:::fact Power and Accountability
The Lamma Power Station supplies electricity to Hong Kong Island's 570,000 customers, none of whom live on Lamma. The island has no air quality monitoring station. Hong Kong's 18 government operated stations are all located on Hong Kong Island and Kowloon. Coal dust has settled on Lamma rooftops and laundry lines since 1982. Long term residents report that natural springs once producing drinkable water have been contaminated, and marine species visible from the shoreline thirty years ago have disappeared.
:::

they supply electricity to 570,000 people on Hong Kong Island
who will never set foot on Lamma,
who will never smell the coal dust that settles on rooftops and laundry lines here,
who will never know that there is no ==air quality monitoring station=={fact: Hong Kong operates 18 air quality monitoring stations, all located on Hong Kong Island, Kowloon, and the New Territories mainland. None are located on any outlying island. Lamma, which hosts a 3,617 MW power station within 500 metres of residential areas, has no monitoring.}
on this island
despite the plant that powers their air conditioners.

there is no air quality monitor.
there is no hospital.
there is no public housing.
there is no ==marine park designation=={fact: WWF Hong Kong has recommended South Lamma be designated a marine park for over two decades. The waters contain 73 species of marine fish including 23 locally rare species, the endangered Indo Pacific finless porpoise, and the only green sea turtle nesting site in Hong Kong. The last confirmed turtle nesting was in 2012. No designation has been made.}
despite two decades of scientific recommendations
and 73 species of marine fish in the surrounding waters,
including the only nesting population of endangered green sea turtles in Hong Kong.
there are no streetlights on the hillside paths.
there are no handrails on many of the steep concrete stairs
that lead to the village houses set into the slope.
there are no private cars,
which sounds idyllic
until you realize it also means no ambulances.

the most important things about Lamma
are the things that are not there.


# the banyan republic

but then you reach the banyan tree,
and the island changes register entirely.

it stands at the waterfront in Yung Shue Wan,
a massive ==*Ficus microcarpa*=={fact: Ficus microcarpa, commonly called the Chinese banyan or Malayan banyan, is the species after which Yung Shue Wan is named. The Cantonese name 榕樹灣 translates directly as Banyan Tree Bay. The species is notable for its aerial roots, which descend from branches and eventually root in the ground, allowing the tree to expand horizontally over centuries. Mature specimens can shade areas exceeding 500 square metres.}
whose aerial roots cascade in thick curtains
from branches that extend fifteen metres in every direction.
the village is named for this species:
榕樹灣, Banyan Tree Bay.
someone has hung potted plants from the roots
in coloured plastic pots, green and coral and blue,
turning the tree into a living vertical garden.
the trunk sits on a raised plinth of cracked pink tile,
and around its base the real life of the island unfolds:
an elderly man at a folding table,
plastic stools arranged in no particular pattern,
metal chairs, a green umbrella.
across the water, houses climb the opposite hillside in pastels.

beneath a second banyan,
or perhaps the same one seen from the other side,
a makeshift outdoor kitchen extends under the canopy.
white plastic tables.
folding chairs.
a corrugated metal roof lashed to bamboo poles with wire.
blue crates stacked.
a gas canister near a cooking area.
this is not a restaurant in any licensed sense.
it is hospitality at the speed of need.
the entire structure could be dismantled in an afternoon
and rebuilt by evening.
it has the temporary permanence of a place that has existed,
in one form or another,
for longer than any building nearby.

:::fact The Banyan as Infrastructure
The banyan predates the power station, the ferry pier, and the sewage system that is only now being installed. In a place with almost no designed public realm, the tree functions as shade, gathering space, and informal public square at the same time. It acts like a plaza, a community centre, and a covered market without official designation.
:::


# three storeys and a thousand improvisations

the ==law says buildings on Lamma cannot exceed three storeys.=={fact: Under Hong Kong's Small House Policy and New Territories Ordinance, indigenous male villagers are entitled to build a single three storey village house of up to 700 square feet per floor, approximately 65 square metres, on their ancestral land. This height restriction applies across New Territories village zones including Lamma Island and is enforced by the Lands Department.}
the buildings obey.
and then they improvise.

every village house in Yung Shue Wan
pushes to the legal ceiling
and then sprouts something extra at the roofline:
glass canopies, metal frame extensions, makeshift greenhouses,
rooftop terraces where the scaffolding poles are still visible
because removing them would mean admitting the addition is finished,
which might attract the attention of someone with a clipboard.
the facades are clad in ==ceramic mosaic tile=={fact: Small format ceramic mosaic tile, typically 2 by 2 centimetres, became the dominant cladding material for New Territories village houses built in the 1980s and 1990s. It was favoured for its low cost, weather resistance, and ease of application without skilled labour. It requires no repainting and resists Hong Kong's humid subtropical climate. The tiles are typically tan, terracotta, or pink.}
tan or terracotta or pink,
the material of choice for Hong Kong village houses
in the 1980s and 1990s
because it was cheap, weatherproof, and required no maintenance.
air conditioning units are bolted to every exterior wall.
wrought iron gates in traditional Chinese patterns guard the entrances.
potted dracaena and palm trees soften the edges.
on one balcony, a row of small ceramic duck figurines faces the street,
a gesture of domesticity so specific it stops you mid stride.

:::fact How Modern Lamma Was Made
These houses are the product of a very particular Hong Kong logic. Indigenous landowners in the New Territories were granted the right to build a single three storey house on their ancestral land, and many built them not to live in but to rent out. When the power station arrived in 1982, it brought workers, then infrastructure, then foreigners attracted by rents that were a fraction of Hong Kong Island prices. The village houses filled with British hippies, then European artists, then journalists and musicians and teachers and anyone who wanted a different rhythm. By the 2021 census, Lamma was 55% Chinese, 23% white European, and 11.4% Filipino. Sixty nationalities on thirteen square kilometres.
:::

:::fact Gentrification
Rents on Lamma have roughly doubled in recent years. A house that cost HK$4,000 per month now commands HK$8,000 to HK$9,000. The Financial Times noted as early as 2013 that Lamma's reputation was shifting from hippy haven to commuter suburb for higher paid professionals. The bohemians who made Lamma desirable are being priced out by the desirability they created.
:::

but the rents are rising.
a house that cost four thousand a month now commands nine.
the bohemians who made Lamma desirable
are being priced out by the desirability they created.
a café with white awnings and an espresso machine
has opened near the power station trail.
two hundred metres away,
a single storey mosaic tile house with a tattered awning
still operates a free bookshelf on its front steps,
a dozen paperbacks in an open plywood case,
available to anyone who walks past.
no lock.
no camera.
no payment terminal.
just trust.


# the honour system and its enemies

on a rock beside the footpath,
someone has placed a hand lettered sign:
"Fresh Blue Butterfly Pea Seeds,
HK$10 per pack, 6 to 10 seeds in each pack,
南丫島 Lamma Island."
a small woven basket holds the seed packets.
a ceramic jar serves as a money box.
there is no vendor.
there is no shop.
there is a bet
that the community is still small enough,
still intimate enough,
still bound by enough mutual recognition
that a stranger will leave ten dollars
and take one packet of seeds and walk on.

:::fact Social Infrastructure and the Honour Economy
This is the last functioning trace of a social structure that predates modern Lamma entirely. The island's original community was organised around surname based kinship networks, with families sharing labour and produce communally. When the demographic mix changed, these networks did not disappear. They opened. The Hans Andersen Club, a community organisation operating from a centre on the island, developed a Time Bank called Hands in Hands, a mobile app built in collaboration with the University of Hong Kong. Residents exchange skills and mutual aid across generations and ethnicities. The app formalises what the butterfly pea basket does informally. It makes reciprocity visible without making it transactional.
:::

but the honour system depends on a ratio.
it works when most people on the path are known quantities.
it falters when the weekend ferry disgorges two hundred strangers
every thirty minutes
and the path becomes a tourist corridor
and the basket on the rock is just another thing to photograph.
nobody steals the seeds.
but nobody buys them either.
they simply become content.


# the beach at the end of the world

past the power station,
past the construction barriers,
past the point where the trail narrows to a thread
between excavated earth and wild lantana,
the island opens.
the trees part.
the concrete ends.
and suddenly there is a beach.

the sand is pale.
the water is calm and silver blue.
mountains across the channel rise in dark green silhouettes.
two people are in the water.
one wades.
one paddles a kayak.
a third sits alone on the sand.
at the tideline, driftwood and a few pieces of plastic catch the last light.

this is the image the tourism board uses.
this is the escape narrative.
and it is not false.
the beach is beautiful.
the quiet is real.
the mountains across the water are genuinely wild.

:::fact Marine Ecology
In these waters, somewhere beneath the kayaker's hull, endangered green sea turtles once nested every summer at Sham Wan beach on the southern coast. The last confirmed nesting was 2012. Recreational boats now anchor in the bay on weekends, up to seventeen at a time during peak season, generating noise levels that deter nesting females. The Indo Pacific finless porpoise, classified as vulnerable by the IUCN, passes through these channels. Romer's tree frog, one of the world's smallest amphibians and found only in Hong Kong, lives in the forest above. Seventy three species of marine fish inhabit the surrounding reef, including 23 locally rare species. None of this is visible from the beach. The ecological richness is as invisible as the sewage infrastructure was for forty years before the government finally broke ground. WWF Hong Kong has called for South Lamma to be designated a marine park, with vessel speed restrictions, non anchoring zones, and controlled human access. No designation has been made.
:::


# time moves sideways here

in a normal city,
the past is buried beneath the present.
old buildings are demolished.
new ones rise on the same footprint.
history is vertical,
layered in the soil like archaeological strata.

on Lamma, time moves sideways.
you walk from a banyan tree that may predate colonial Hong Kong,
past a 1970s mosaic tile cottage with a mandala tapestry on the wall,
past a three storey village house from 1988
with rooftop scaffolding and ceramic ducks,
past a renovated café from 2023 with glass walls and artisanal coffee,
past an open trench where workers are laying ==sewage pipes=={fact: PWP No. 4355DS is retrofitting infrastructure that was never built when the village houses were constructed in the 1970s and 1980s. The government acknowledged that untreated sewage had been polluting Lamma's watercourses and coastal waters for decades before the 2021 project commenced.}
that should have been installed in the 1980s,
past a rock with a basket of butterfly pea seeds and a ceramic money jar,
and down to a beach where ==endangered turtles=={fact: Green sea turtles, Chelonia mydas, nested annually at Sham Wan on Lamma's southern coast. The last confirmed nesting was in 2012. The beach is closed to the public from June 1 to October 31 each year to protect nesting habitat, but no turtles have been recorded nesting for over a decade.}
no longer come.

every era is present on the same two metre path.
nothing has been demolished;
nothing has been curated.

the island simply walks around its own past,
the way you walk around a fallen tree on a trail,
and carries on.
the ==power station chimneys=={fact: Despite being a major source of coal dust and environmental contamination, Lamma's three power station chimneys have become an unlikely cultural landmark. Residents describe using them as a navigational reference when approaching by ferry from Central. They are simultaneously the largest source of pollution on the island and the most reliable landmark for coming home.}
defining the skyline
are only forty years old,
but they already feel ancient,
the way anything becomes ancient
when a community absorbs it into its navigational grammar.
residents use the chimneys to orient the ferry approach from the sea:
they are simultaneously the largest source of pollution on the island,
and the most reliable landmark for coming home.

this is the paradox the island lives inside,
and the paradox your body learns as you walk the trail:
that beauty and contamination share every frame.
that the café and the construction trench are neighbours.
that the free bookshelf and the rising rents exist on the same street.
that the honour system and the tourist economy use the same footpath.
that the ferry brings both community and its dissolution,
every thirty minutes,
from dawn until 11:30 PM,
when the last boat leaves
and the island finally belongs to the people who remain,
standing in the darkness where there are no streetlights,
listening to the harbour water slap against the hulls of boats
in a rhythm that sounds, in the silence, like breathing.
