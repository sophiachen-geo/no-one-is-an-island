She was born Lin Mo on Meizhou Island in 960 CE, the seventh daughter of a fisherman. She died young — some say at 28, some say she simply ascended. Within a century the coastal communities of Fujian were worshipping her as the protector of sailors.

A thousand years later, her name is on a Taiwanese military outpost twenty kilometres from her birthplace. Nangan, Beigan, Dongju, Xiju — the Matsu Islands are named for her. Fishermen prayed to her long before the soldiers arrived.

In Hong Kong's Aberdeen typhoon shelter, there is a Tin Hau temple squeezed between floating restaurants. Tin Hau is her Cantonese name. The Tanka boat people, who spent their entire lives on the water, kept her close.

On Lamma's waterfront at Sok Kwu Wan, her temple is red and gold between the seafood restaurants. On Cheung Chau, her presence overlaps with Pak Tai and a dozen other maritime gods.

I visited her birthplace last, on Meizhou. The ancestral temple faces the sea. A 14-metre stone statue stands at the island's tip, looking toward Taiwan. Every year, thousands of Taiwanese cross the strait to worship here. The water between them is the same water she crossed.
