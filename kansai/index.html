新宮 Shingū

One small city, planned at six scales at once — and none of the boundaries agree.

The basic regional-planning idea is simple to state: a compact regional hub on the coast, smaller service nodes, rural “small hubs” tied together by transport, hazard-sensitive land use — and, underneath it all, a river basin that crosses prefectural boundaries. So the question is never only where should houses go? Scroll, and the map moves between the scales at which Shingū is actually being planned.

↓ scroll

0

A tiny city at the bottom of a very large map

Shingū (新宮) sits where the Kumano River meets the Pacific, on the Wakayama side of a river that is also the border with Mie. Wakayama, Nara and Mie all meet in the mountains behind it.

The planning problem runs at several overlapping scales at once: a regional centre, a polycentric compact city, a hazard map, a pre-disaster reconstruction plan, a three-prefecture river basin, a transport network and a sacred landscape. Each scale has its own map.

1

Shingū is planned as the centre of a larger Kumano region

Wakayama’s Higashimuro regional master plan (東牟婁圏域都市計画区域マスタープラン) names Shingū the regional centre. The surrounding towns are not expected to duplicate every high-order function. Shingū concentrates major medical care, commerce, administration and employment. Nachikatsuura, Taiji, Kozagawa and Kushimoto keep their own local-service, tourism, fishing and settlement roles. Shingū’s own plan calls its centre a hub for the wider Kumano region.

So spatially it reads mountain villages and coastal towns → Shingū, not a self-contained municipality.

The prefecture’s official 新宮 secondary medical area is exactly the six Higashimuro municipalities: 922 km², and it stops at the river. A modelled 60-minute drive from the Shingū Medical Centre ( gold) does not stop there. It crosses into Kihō and Mihama in Mie . Medical areas: MLIT A38 (2020). Drive time: OSM roads at assumed speeds; a sketch of reach, not a service-area study.

2

Inside Shingū: a polycentric compact city

The Location Optimization Plan (立地適正化計画) exists because a shrinking population lets services diffuse and thin out. So the city names tiers of nodes:

  • Central hub — 緑丘・城南 Midorigaoka–Jōnan: the station and city centre, meant to keep the widest range of urban functions.
  • Regional hub — 光洋 Kōyō: the Shingū Municipal Medical Center and the southern urban area, with Miwasaki.
  • Local / living hubs — 熊野川町 Kumanogawa and 高田 Takata: mountain communities where the aim is not urbanisation but basic accessibility and a viable community life.

The principle: concentrate services, don’t abandon the periphery. The outlying settlements stay connected to the centre by public transport, so this is a hub-and-network model.

Of a 256 km² municipality, the plan area is 16.8 km². The residential-inducement zone (居住誘導区域) covers 4.6 km². The three urban-function zones (都市機能誘導区域) cover 1.9 km². Zones: MLIT A55, Shingū City (FY2024 edition).

3

The compact-city line is drawn around hazard

The residential-inducement zone is defined by transport access, existing infrastructure and population density. Then the city explicitly excludes landslide hazard zones and danger locations, areas from which tsunami evacuation is especially difficult, and unsuitable land. When Wakayama later designated more 土砂災害特別警戒区域 (landslide red zones), Shingū amended the plan and cut them out.

This is not compulsory retreat. It is slow spatial steering: new housing and facilities outside the preferred zones trigger a planning notification, and incentives pull development inside. The geography is meant to change over decades, not by relocating everyone tomorrow.

Only 0.4% of the residential zone overlaps a designated red zone. That is boundary noise between datasets; the zone’s edge stops where the red begins. Landslide zones: MLIT A33 (designations as of Aug 2025).

4

The contradiction: the centre itself is exposed

The places that already have the railway, commerce, municipal services, housing, heritage and accumulated infrastructure are partly low-lying coastal and riverine land. So planners cannot say “hazard map red = abandon everything”. That would destroy the functioning regional centre.

Shingū instead tries to reconcile compactness, accessibility, existing investment and cultural continuity with tsunami, river flooding and internal-drainage flooding. It combines land-use steering with vertical evacuation, high-ground refuge, drainage pumps, levees, evacuation roads, hazard mapping and pre-disaster reconstruction planning. The 2025 Tsunami Disaster-Resilient Community Development Plan (津波防災地域づくり推進計画) is meant to line up with the Urban Master Plan, the Location Optimization Plan, the resilience plan and the municipal strategy.

86% of the central urban-function zone lies inside the maximum-assumed (L2) flood area of the Kumano and Ichida rivers, and 30% inside the L2 tsunami area. Half of the residential zone (51%) is in one or the other. The Kōyō zone around the Medical Centre is in neither. The polycentric plan doubles as redundancy: the second hub is the dry one. Flood: MLIT A31a, Kumano/Ichida/Aidani, 想定最大規模. Tsunami: MLIT A40 (Wakayama/Mie inundation assumptions). Shares computed from polygon overlap.

5

Reconstruction, planned before the disaster

Wakayama’s regional urban-planning policy does not stop at earthquake happens → rebuild. It calls for 高台移転, high-ground relocation, and for deciding the post-disaster land-use structure before the Nankai Trough earthquake. The question it asks: if the current coastal settlement is destroyed, where should the future city be? That is pre-disaster recovery planning written into ordinary land-use planning.

The contours (20 m and 50 m) show why the question is hard. The inundation reaches the foot of the hills almost everywhere. Above it the ground rises fast, and much of the nearest slope is already mapped as landslide zone. High ground exists; flat, safe, reachable high ground is scarce.

6

Now throw away the municipal map

Hydrologically, the relevant region is not Shingū City. It is Nara + Mie + Wakayama → the Kumano/Shingū River basin → Shingū at the outlet. The basin-wide flood-resilience programme (流域治水) involves national government, three prefectures, many municipalities, hydropower companies, dam operators, forestry, sabō (sediment-control) agencies and local communities.

The toolbox is more than levees: river excavation, stronger embankments, ring levees, forest management, sabō, coordinated dam operation, rainwater storage, drainage pumps, land-use controls, high-ground urban development, evacuation planning and integrated surface/river flood mapping. This is regional planning as risk-system management.

The basin covers 2,365 km² (the official figure is 2,360): 63% Nara, 22% Wakayama, 16% Mie. Shingū City holds 10% of the land that drains through it, and the whole Higashimuro region only 13%. The water system’s statutory name is still 新宮川水系, the Shingū River system: it is named after the city at its mouth. Basin delineated from SRTM (priority-flood, D8) with the OSM coastline as the sea mask; shares from N03 boundaries.

7

Climate change is already written into the structure

The River Basin Flood Management 2.0 documents (流域治水プロジェクト2.0) use the national planning assumptions for a +2 °C world:

×1.1rainfall
→
×1.2peak discharge
→
×2flood frequency

The amplification is the argument. A 10% nudge in rain becomes a doubling in how often the design flood arrives, and levees are sized to a number that moves. So the basin programme pairs physical works with land-use regulation and with changes in where and how people live. Climate adaptation leaves the environment department and enters regional land-use and infrastructure planning.

8

Transport planning is resilience planning

Shingū’s 2023–2027 Regional Public Transport Plan exists partly because a shrinking, ageing population can no longer support conventional transit everywhere. It has already introduced demand-responsive taxis in Kumanogawa, redesigned transport in Takata and adjusted urban bus routes, all to keep dispersed settlements tied to the service centre. At the regional scale, the Kisei corridor highways change accessibility between Shingū, Nachikatsuura and the rest of the peninsula.

Within Higashimuro, 61% of National Route 42 (40 of 66 km) and 56% of the JR Kisei Line (37 of 66 km) run through the L2 tsunami inundation area. On the new expressway sections it is 1 of 16 km. A road here is evacuation route, hospital access, supply chain and post-disaster redundancy at once. Locally the expressway is called 命の道, “the road of life”.

9

The mountains are not empty space waiting for development

The Higashimuro plan restrains inappropriate urban expansion into farmland and forest and protects the landscape of the Kumano Kodō and its cultural surroundings. That produces a real tension. Preserve the forest and sacred landscape; keep tiny mountain communities alive and reachable; avoid dangerous slopes; don’t sprawl — and yet possibly move exposed coastal functions uphill.

The pilgrimage routes converge on the three Grand Shrines: Hongū, Hayatama in Shingū, and Nachi. Between Hongū and Shingū the Kumano River itself is the route; the river is inscribed as a pilgrimage way in the 2004 World Heritage listing. For its lower course the same channel is the flood corridor, the Wakayama–Mie border and a World Heritage path. Nakahechi and Kohechi from OpenStreetMap; Ōmine Okugake, Iseji and Ohechi drawn as approximate courses through waypoints.

10

The functional region ignores the prefectural border

Immediately across the Kumano River is Kihō, in Mie Prefecture; farther upstream are communities in Mie and Nara. Economically and geographically they form one connected system, yet ordinary urban planning is administered separately by different prefectures and municipalities.

Water forces them to collaborate anyway. That is why the Kumano basin may be a more coherent planning geography than the administrative region.

The national flood model floods both banks alike (blue). The land-use tools do not: the residential-inducement zone (teal) is Shingū’s and ends at the water, and the Kihō bank is planned by another town in another prefecture. Even the tsunami inundation maps for the two banks come from two different prefectural models. Upstream, Kitayama village shows the same logic in reverse: it is a Wakayama exclave surrounded by Nara and Mie. The usual explanation is that its timber floated down the river to Shingū.

11

Different problems produce different “correct” regions

  • Healthcare & commerce — the Kumano region around the Shingū centre (functional, no legal line)
  • Urban planning — Shingū’s city-planning area, 16.8 km²
  • Provincial planning — Higashimuro, 922 km², stopping at the prefectural border
  • Flood risk — the three-prefecture Kumano basin, 2,365 km²
  • Cultural landscape — the Kii Mountains and pilgrimage network: a network, not an area
  • Tsunami risk — the Pacific-facing coastal strip

None of these boundaries are the same. Only the two planning regions carry legal force; the other four are where the problems actually live. The city is tiny, but the planning problem is not.

The model, as a valley section

Mountain communities ↘ small local hubs ↘ the Shingū urban area ↘ the other Higashimuro centres, with the tsunami, watershed, landslide/sabō and cultural-landscape geographies laid over the whole thing. Here it is drawn along the real long profile of the Totsukawa–Kumano, from the Ōmine headwaters to the mouth and then down the coast, in the manner of Geddes’s valley section.
